## Introduction

To start a discussion on the concept that is commonly translated as "rebirth" or "reincarnation", I
believe it necessary to start by presenting the original Pali and Sanskrit terms and to offer a new
translation that is the title of this essay, "rebecoming". The original Sanskrit term is
*punarbhava* and its Pali equivalent is *punabbhava*. Both of these terms are made up of two roots,
*punar* which means "again" and *bhava* which means "becoming" or "existence". Therefore, a literal
translation of these terms would be "becoming again", hence my choice of "rebecoming".

The term is commonly translated as "rebirth" and less commonly as "reincarnation". The first term is
advanced by those who think the existing term reincarnation too closely associates the Buddhist
concept of *punarbhava* with the idea of a permanent soul or self that transmigrates from one life
to the next. Such connotations exist because of the belief that a permanent soul or self is
necessary for any process that involves a continuity of existence across multiple lives. Dependent
origination, the central teaching of Buddhism, explains in detail how such continuity can exist
without a permanent soul or self. The original term must have been deliberately chosen to avoid such
connotations because the term used in Hinduism for this process, one involving a permanent self, is
*punarjanma*, which literally means "born again". This essay will use the term "rebecoming" when
referring to the Buddhist concept of *punarbhava* from now on. The translation "rebirth" cannot then
remove the connotations of "reincarnation" it was meant to avoid. The term "rebirth" will be used
when explicitly referring to the birth of a new being in a new body to avoid awkward sentences.  

The doctrine of rebecoming is central to understanding Buddhism and without it, its goal of putting
an end to the beginningless cycle of rebecoming becomes outright incoherent. For most of Buddhist
history, from the time of the Buddha until its encounter with modernity, such simple logic stopped
teachers or scholars from questioning that the Buddha taught this doctrine literally. The situation
is very different today, specially in places where Buddhism has not taken root as a native religion,
and the doctrine is often marginalized, misrepresented, or outright denied as something that the
Buddha never taught.

This essay will present a defense of the doctrine of rebecoming as a central part of the Buddhist
path, refute any notion that the Buddha did not teach it or meant it allegorically, address the
common misconceptions that are used to advance such notions, explain the mechanism that allows for a
continuity of mind beyond death without a permanent self, and cover the sources of knowledge that
Buddhist epistemology recognizes as valid means of verifying rebecoming for oneself. Since the tasks
ahead will take plenty of space, this essay will not cover the process by which denials of
rebecoming came to be so common. Those interested in that topic can refer to the book "The Making of
Buddhist Modernism" by David McMahan.

The goal of this essay is to provide clarity to those who wonder about the role and centrality of
rebecoming in Buddhism, not to condition anyone to accept it. Those who disagree should follow the
Buddha's advice and search for a different path that better fits them. Teaching a version of
Buddhism that rejects or marginalizes rebecoming serves neither the Dharma nor spiritual seekers.

## Wrong Views

Denials of rebecoming have not been stopped by the fact that the Buddha explicitly taught that such
denials prevent the very first step of the path, which is to have a right view about the path. Many
such teachings exist, but this section will use ["The Great Forty"
](https://suttacentral.net/mn117/en/sujato) sutta as its basis. The Buddha cannot be more explicit
at denying the notion that there is no afterlife than when he starts the bulk of his discourse by
saying:

> And what is wrong view? ‘There’s no meaning in giving, sacrifice, or offerings. There’s no fruit
> or result of good and bad deeds. There’s no afterlife. There’s no such thing as mother and father,
> or beings that are reborn spontaneously. And there’s no ascetic or brahmin who is rightly
> comported and rightly practiced, and who describes the afterlife after realizing it with their own
> insight.’ This is wrong view.

The above passage needs only a couple clarifications. The reference about beings reborn
spontaneously is a reference to believing in other realms of existence beyond the animal and human
realms that are visible to the naked eye. The reference to a rightly comported and rightly practiced
ascetic or brahmin is a reference to the Buddha himself. The rest is self-explanatory. Since the
Buddha starts by defining wrong view before defining right view, this section will start by
debunking common strategies for denying rebecoming. The position that the Buddha did not teach
rebecoming at all is sadly not uncommon, but it can be outright dismissed by the passage above. More
commonly, the denials tend to be more subtle and appear logical to those unfamiliar.

### The Buddha merely used the language available in his time metaphorically

One of the most common strategies for denying rebecoming is to say that the Buddha merely used the
language available in his time to explain concepts that were beyond what his audience could
understand. We have seen already how the original term for rebecoming is different from the term
used in Hinduism, so the argument is weak from the start. There is an even more fundamental problem
with this proposal, for it assumes that the concepts required for teaching that no afterlife exists
and that life ends at death were not available to him. Not only were they available, they are used
by him in the context of denying them. The terms include those translated to "nihilism",
"materialism", "annihilationism", and others. The specifics of such denials will be covered in more
detail in the rest of the section.

### Rebecoming is a process that happens moment to moment in this life

Another strategy is to say that the Buddha taught rebecoming but only as a process that happens
moment to moment in this life. This works for many people because it is true that the Buddha taught
rebecoming as a process that happens moment to moment in this life. However, the passage above shows
that denying the process stops at death was outright rejected by the Buddha as a wrong view. The
specifics of dependent origination, the mechanism that allows for a continuity of mind beyond death,
will be covered in more detail later. Suffice to say for now the Buddha speaks clearly about the
parts that have to do with birth, aging, and death as processes happening in the future.

Usually in tandem with teaching that rebecoming is only a moment to moment process, rebecoming is
taught as a psychological process. The six realms of existence are not physical places, but only
mental states characterized by certain emotions or attitudes. The second assertion is not entirely
wrong, as we can clearly see that some humans live in extremely negative mental states that are very
similar to the hell realms, while others live in the opposite extremes. However, the explicit
mention of spontaneous rebirth and of the fruit of good and bad deeds in the passage above shows
that the Buddha already saw these objections coming.

The range of bad and good actions is simply too broad to allow for their fruit to be experienced in
the body of a human or an animal. As a singular example, the highest beings in samsara, the beings
in the formless deva realms, are said to have no physical body, no sense organs, and to spend
billions of years absorbed in deep and refined states of meditation. Such sublime existence comes
about from spending a long time meditating on very similar states of concentration because rebirth
as a human or an animal is simply incongruent as a fruit of such actions.

### The Buddha did not teach rebecoming because he was scientific and rational 

A central pillar of Buddhist modernism is the idea of the Buddha as a scientific and rational
explorer of the mind. The history of this idea is beyond the scope of this essay, but it reflects
the ideals of the Enlightenment and the scientific revolution. Hidden in this idea lies the
unexplored assumption that the Buddha was a materialist scientist who discovered mind came from
matter, saw the idea of an afterlife as a superstition, but perhaps kept the language of his day to
help the primitive people of his time.

The assumption that the Buddha lacked the proper language was already explored. Not only did he have
the proper language, similar ideas to modern day materialism were already present and popular in the
cultural milieu of his time. The Cārvāka school taught that a human beings are made up of four
physical elements and that when they die their mind is annihilated. The Buddha explicitly refuted
this view and the compilers of the suttas included it in the
[second](https://suttacentral.net/dn2/en/sujato) of his long discourses.

### The Buddha was agnostic and allowed Buddhists to discard the concept

The [Kālāma sutta](https://suttacentral.net/an3.65/en/sujato) is often cited as evidence that the
Buddha allowed his followers to discard concepts they found unpalatable and to rely on their own
experience to determine what is true. Such readings of the suttas ignore that each has a specific
context and audience, in this case a group of people who had received many contradictory teachings
and wanted some clarity on how to determine which teachings to follow.

The Buddha gives the advice that is commonly quoted as a license to discard his teachings:

> “So, Kālāmas, when I said: ‘Please, don’t go by oral transmission, don’t go by lineage, don’t go
> by testament, don’t go by canonical authority, don’t rely on logic, don’t rely on inference, don’t
> go by reasoned train of thought, don’t go by the acceptance of a view after deliberation, don’t go
> by the appearance of competence, and don’t think “The ascetic is our respected teacher.” But when
> you know for yourselves:
>
> “These things are skillful, blameless, praised by sensible people, and when you undertake them,
> they lead to welfare and happiness”, then you should acquire them and keep them.’ That’s what I
> said, and this is why I said it.

Mentions of this quote often are paired with the claim that Buddhism is a path that encourages
rational and scientific inquiry as the measure of proof, even though the quote explicitly
discourages relying on logic, inference, and reasoned train of thought. The second paragraph is
often conveniently left out, for it emphasizes the importance of personal experience and direct
perception and what one should do after such experience. The Buddha says to acquire and keep them
wholesale, not to discard and keep parts based on personal preferences.

The Buddha then goes on to teach his path and the possible rewards of following it, mentioning being
reborn in a good place as a consolation that can be obtained if it turns out there is another world
and good and bad deeds have a result. The hedging language serves his purpose of teaching the path
without alienating those who do not already believe in rebecoming, but there is no evidence from his
teaching to his own followers that he harbors any doubts.

Those who investigate his teachings and come to the conclusion that there is no rebecoming should
follow his advice, discard his teachings as incomplete or incorrect, and look for a different path
that better serves them. No accurate reading of this sutta supports the idea that one can discard
core concepts of the Buddha's teachings, claim to still be following them, and propagate the
resulting mishmash as what the Buddha really taught.

### Rebecoming is not essential to the path and irrelevant for practice

Those who concede it is impossible to deny that the Buddha taught rebecoming, still have one last
line of defense: rebecoming is not essential to the path and irrelevant for practice because it has
little to do with producing an end to suffering. Debunking this claim is straightforward once the
very meaning of what is being translated as "suffering" is understood.

The general gist of the Buddha's story is that he was born into a life of luxury, where he spent the
first three decades of his life purposely protected from the harsh realities of life. After
venturing out of his palace for the first time, he witnessed the suffering of sickness, old age, and
death and was inspired to leave his comfortable life for a solution to such suffering.

After years of failed search, he sat under the Bodhi tree, resolved to stay there until he found a
solution, and attained enlightenment. It was during this night that he unraveled the net of causes
and conditions and saw how he had lived innumerable previous lives, each the result of his previous
actions. Far from being a comforting thought, he understood that all beings are trapped in a cycle
of rebecoming. As he continued the unraveling, he saw how the cycle is fueled by *dukkha*, the
clinging to the aggregates that make up a sentient being, and the term that is commonly translated
as "suffering". Through further unraveling, he realized the root cause of *dukkha* to be the
ignorance that these aggregates are impermanent, unsatisfactory, and lack any permanent self one can
cling to. Ignorance creates a deluded habit of creating a self where there is none that leads to
clinging to the aggregates and to the cycle of rebecoming. Therefore, claiming that rebecoming is an
optional part on the path to ending suffering misunderstands the very nature of the suffering the
path is meant to end.

Usually paired with that claim is the idea that rebecoming is irrelevant because it has no practical
implications and one can sidestep the question by merely practicing. This view of practice as an
exercise divorced from the view tends to appear in some Zen circles, where the view is dismissed as
either irrelevant or secondary to the practice of just sitting. While it might be true that Zen
practices and texts place less emphasis on rebecoming than other traditions, Zen is fundamentally a
Mahayana tradition and as such never denies the doctrine of rebecoming. The lesser emphasis exists
because Zen was developed as a vehicle that could result in a more direct and immediate realization
of the path, allowing the best practitioners to complete the path in a single lifetime. Given that
most practitioners do not belong to this category, the lesser emphasis on rebecoming does not serve
their needs and capacities.

Not only is rebecoming needed to complete the path, the initial attainments that firmly establish
one on the path to becoming an arahat (the result of Theravada practice) or a Buddha (the result of
Mahayana practice) imply that any doubts about rebecoming have been dispelled. The first of the four
stages of awakening in Theravada is called stream-entry, and it is defined as the stage where one
has personally experienced their lack of a self and thus eradicated any doubts about the Buddha's
teachings. The first of the ten stages of a Bodhisattva in Mahayana comes through a direct
realization of the emptiness of all phenomena, an experience that dispels any doubts concerning the
mechanisms of rebecoming. Not only is rebecoming required for the end of the path, it is also
required for making any significant progress.

### An afterlife is impossible because the Buddha taught the absence of self

A common question beginners have is how an afterlife can be possible if the Buddha taught the
absence of a self. The question is so common that one can find people asking it every day in online
forums. That beginners have this question is not surprising, but that some people claiming to be
teachers of Buddhism fall under the same misconception is quite concerning. As was mentioned in the
introduction, the word *punarbhava* deliberately avoids the connotations of a permanent self.

Buddhism is often called the "middle way" because it positions itself as a path that avoids the
extremes of eternalism and nihilism. In this context, eternalism is the belief in a permanent soul
or self that transmigrates from one life to the next, and nihilism is the belief that there is no
continuity of existence beyond death. The middle way found by the Buddha straddles the line between
these two extremes by teaching that such continuity of existence is possible without the involvement
of a permanent self that transmigrates from life to life.

Dependent origination is the teaching that explains this process in detail and will be elaborated
later in this essay. Those who teach the supramundane before understanding the mundane contribute to
the degeneration of the teachings despite their best intentions. Just like all other phenomena, the
Buddha taught that his dharma was also subject to impermanence. Nothing but a clear and
uncompromising rejection of such wrong views can slow down the disappearance of the true Dharma from
this world.

## Right View

After condemning wrong views, the Buddha continues by defining right view, which he splits into two,
one provisional (often called "mundane right view") and one ultimate, often called (supramundane
right view). He defines mundane right view as:

> And what is right view that is accompanied by defilements, partakes of good deeds, and ripens in
> attachments? ‘There is meaning in giving, sacrifice, and offerings. There are fruits and results
> of good and bad deeds. There is an afterlife. There are such things as mother and father, and
> beings that are reborn spontaneously. And there are ascetics and brahmins who are rightly
> comported and rightly practiced, and who describe the afterlife after realizing it with their own
> insight.’ This is right view that is accompanied by defilements, partakes of good deeds, and
> ripens in attachments.

There is not much to explain here as it is the opposite of the wrong view that he condemned. He
continues to define supramundane right view as:

> And what is right view that is noble, undefiled, transcendent, a factor of the path? It’s the
> wisdom—the faculty of wisdom, the power of wisdom, the awakening factor of investigation of
> principles, and right view as a factor of the path—in one intent on the noble, intent on the
> undefiled, who possesses the noble path and develops the noble path. This is called right view
> that is noble, undefiled, transcendent, a factor of the path.

Supramundane right view is not defined as a list of beliefs to accept, but as the wisdom that sees
things as they are, the result of cultivating the noble path. By defining two types of right view,
the Buddha provides the concession that modernist, secular, and "pragmatic" movements believe is
missing from traditional Buddhism by acknowledging that the ultimate view is hard to grasp and
giving his followers something to lean on while they work on the other parts of the noble path. One
can adopt mundane right view out of faith or as a wager much like he suggested to the Kālāmas. 

The Buddha acknowledges from the start that mundane right view is not enough by calling it "right
view that is accompanied by defilements, partakes of good deeds, and ripens in attachments". Such
right view is enough to help one maintain ethical conduct, produce good karma, and respect the sages
who explain the path. Through these seeds, the motivation to engage in the cultivation of
supramundane right view is produced, either in this life or in a future one.

The denials in the previous section often follow the opposite strategy of trying to discard and
marginalize mundane right view in favor of its supramundane version. Doing so equates to
explaining gravity to a small child by trying to introduce them to General Relativity instead of the
more approachable concept of objects attracting each other. It is no surprise then that those who
expound on supramundane right while harboring doubts about mundane right view hold views as
confused as those of complete beginners.

### Dependent Origination

The wisdom that sees things as they are, the result of cultivating the noble path, is the wisdom
that understands dependent origination. This teaching is so central to Buddhism that hearing it
caused Śāriputra, one of the Buddha's chief disciples, to attain the first stage of awakening. In
the story, it is summarized as
[follows](https://www.lotsawahouse.org/words-of-the-buddha/essence-of-dependent-origination):

> All phenomena that originate from causes,
>
> The Tathāgata has taught these causes;
>
> And also that which puts a stop to these causes,
>
> This too has been taught by the Great Śramaṇa. 

This section elaborates on the teaching of dependent origination using a sutra from the Tibetan
Canon, the [Rice Seedling Sutra](https://84000.co/translation/toh210). Using a Mahayana sutra serves
the purpose of showing that the teaching of dependent origination shows consistently across all
Buddhist traditions. Many suttas in the Pali Canon explain the teaching in similar ways, such as
[The Great Discourse on Causation](https://suttacentral.net/dn15/en/sujato). Only minimal commentary
will be provided, as the sutra is quite clear on its own. I highly recommend reading it in its
entirety.

The sutra starts when Śāriputra approaches Maitreya and requests that he explains a simile the Buddha
used to explain dependent origination:

> “Maitreya, here today, the Bhagavān, gazing at a rice seedling, spoke this aphorism to the
> bhikṣus: ‘Bhikṣus, whoever sees dependent arising sees the Dharma. Whoever sees the Dharma sees
> the Buddha.’ Having said this, the Bhagavān fell silent. Maitreya, what is the meaning of this
> aphorism spoken by the Sugata?

The centrality of this teaching is such that simply seeing it is equated with seeing the whole of
the Buddha's teachings and with seeing the Buddha himself. Maitreya answers:

> Well, the phrase dependent arising means that something arises because something else already
> exists; something is born because something else was already born. That is to say, ignorance
> causes formations. Formations cause consciousness. Consciousness causes name and form. Name and
> form cause the six sense sources. The six sense sources cause contact. Contact causes sensation.
> Sensation causes craving. Craving causes appropriation. Appropriation causes becoming. Becoming
> causes birth. And birth causes aging and death, sorrow, lamentation, suffering, despair, and
> anxiety. Thus does this entire great heap of suffering arise.

Simply put, dependent origination explains how things can only come through the interdependence of
causes and conditions. The second part of the statement lists the causal chain that leads to the
cycle of rebecoming the Buddha discovered in the night of his enlightenment. The meaning of each
link is explained further in the sutra. Maitreya continues by explaining the Buddha also taught how
to extinguish this cycle:

> When ignorance ceases, formations cease. When formations cease, consciousness ceases. When
> consciousness ceases, name and form cease. When name and form cease, the six sense sources cease.
> When the six sense sources cease, contact ceases. When contact ceases, sensation ceases. When
> sensation ceases, craving ceases. When craving ceases, appropriation ceases. When appropriation
> ceases, becoming ceases. When becoming ceases, birth ceases. And when birth ceases, aging and
> death, sorrow, lamentation, suffering, despair, and anxiety cease. Thus does this entire great
> heap of suffering cease. This is what the Bhagavān has called dependent arising.

After explaining the meaning of the Dharma and the Buddha in terms of dependent origination,
Maitreya defines dependent origination as:

> “Why is it called dependent arising? It is called dependent arising because it is causal and
> conditional, not non-causal and non-conditional. In this connection, the Bhagavān concisely taught
> the characteristics of dependent arising as follows: ‘Results come from their own specific
> conditions. 

He continues to categorize dependent arising in terms of causes, conditions, and an inner and outer
aspects. Rather than quote the entire section, I will focus on the definition of the outer aspect,
in which the mechanism that allows for a continuity of mind beyond death without a permanent self is
clearly explained:

> "Here, outer dependent arising is to be seen in terms of five aspects. What five aspects? As not
> permanent, as not discontinuous, as not involving transmigration, as the production of a large
> result from a small cause, and as a continuity of similar type.
>
> “How is it not permanent? It is not permanent because the sprout and the seed are different. The
> sprout is not the seed. The sprout does not come from the seed after it has ceased, nor does it
> come from the seed while it has not yet ceased.19 Rather, the sprout is born precisely as the seed
> ceases.
>
> “How is it not discontinuous? It is not discontinuous because a sprout is not born from a seed
> that has already ceased, nor from a seed that has not yet ceased. Rather, like the beam of a scale
> tilting from up to down, a sprout is born precisely when the seed has ceased.
> 
> “How does it not involve transmigration? It does not involve transmigration because the sprout and
> the seed are different; that which is the sprout is not the seed.
>
> “How does it entail the producing of a large result from a small cause? A large fruit is produced
> from the planting of a small seed. Therefore, it entails the producing of a large result from a
> small cause.
>
> “Lastly, fruit is produced precisely according to the type of seed planted. Therefore, it involves
> a continuity of similar type.
>
> “Thus is outer dependent arising to be seen in terms of five aspects.

We can now see that the metaphor of the rice seedling is used to explain how the process of
dependent arising applies to external objects without requiring a permanent self. The inner aspect
refers to the causal chain that leads to the continued existence of sentient beings.

> “What, then, is the causal relation in inner dependent arising? It starts with ignorance causing
> formations and so on, until finally, birth causes aging and death. If ignorance does not arise,
> then formations do not manifest and so on, until finally, if birth does not arise, then aging and
> death do not manifest. Likewise, from the existence of ignorance, formations occur and so on,
> until finally, from the existence of birth, comes aging and death.

The bulk of the sutra continues to explain the meaning of each link in the causal chain. Rather than
quote sections for each, which the reader can easily read for themselves, I will focus on the very
first and last links. The first link, ignorance, is explained as:

> “Here, what is ignorance? That which perceives these same six elements to be unitary, whole,
> permanent, constant, eternal, pleasurable, a self, a being, a life force, a creature, a soul, a
> man, an individual, a human, a person, me, and mine, along with the many other such variations of
> misapprehension, is called ignorance. The presence of such ignorance brings desire, aversion, and
> delusion toward objects.

By imputing a permanent self to a set of aggregates where there are none, ignorance sets in motion
the entire chain that ends with:

>  The emergence of the aggregates from such a cause is birth. The maturation of the aggregates
>  after birth is aging. The perishing of the decrepit aggregates is death. The inner torment of the
>  deluded, attached, dying person is sorrow. The utterance that comes from sorrow is lamentation.
>  The experience of discomfort associated with the collection of the five consciousnesses is
>  suffering. The mental suffering accompanied by attention is despair. Moreover, any other subtle
>  defilements of this kind are called anxiety.

In case anyone thinks this causal chain is only limited to one life, Maitreya clarifies that:

> “Thus, this twelvefold dependent arising—which comes from several different causes and from
> several different conditions, is neither permanent nor impermanent, is neither compounded nor
> uncompounded, is not without any cause or condition, is not an experiencer, and is not something
> exhaustible, something destructible, or something that ceases‍—has proceeded from time immemorial,
> without interruption, like the flow of a river.
>
> Nevertheless, there are four links that serve as the cause for assembling this twelvefold
> dependent arising. What four links? Namely, ignorance, craving, karma, and consciousness.
>
> “Consciousness functions as a cause by having the nature of a seed. Karma functions as a cause by
> having the nature of a field. Ignorance and craving function as causes by having the nature of
> afflictions.

In summary, the ignorance that assigns a permanent self to our experience sets in motion a chain of
causes and conditions that leads to consciousness appropriating a set of material aggregates. At
death, consciousness separates from these aggregates and, like a seed, continues the process of
rebecoming by taking a new set of aggregates. Maitreya summarizes it better than I ever could:

> In the same way, although things are devoid of owner, devoid of ownership, ungraspable,
> space-like, and their nature is the mark of illusion, because there is no deficiency of requisite
> causes and conditions, the seed of consciousness born of karma and afflictions will nonetheless
> produce the sprout of name and form within whichever mother’s womb one will take rebirth through.

## Verifying Rebecoming 

Since the Buddha taught that rebecoming is a verifiable process, the last section of this essay
covers the valid sources of knowledge recognized by Buddhist epistemology that can be used to verify
it for oneself. Epistemology studies the nature of knowledge and how it can be acquired. There is
some variation within the tradition on which sources of knowledge are valid, but this section will
cover the testimony of reliable sources, inference, and direct perception, which tend to be the most
commonly accepted ones. 

### Testimony of Reliable Sources

The testimony of reliable sources is not as always accepted as a separate valid source but as a
category of inference. It means accepting parts of a teaching based on the trustworthiness of the
source. In his discourse to the Kālāmas, the Buddha encouraged them to navigate the many views to
which they had been exposed by trying them out and accepting those that are skillful, blameless,
praised by sensible people, and lead to welfare and happiness.

Those who wish to try to verify rebecoming through the testimony of reliable sources can start by
temporarily suspending their disbelief, accepting mundane right view as a wager, and moving on to
practice the next steps of the path. If their progress leads them to declare that the parts of the
path they can verify for themselves are skillful and lead to welfare and happiness, it will be
natural to infer that the parts they cannot yet verify for themselves, such as rebecoming, also
possess the same qualities. 

### Inference

The main valid source of knowledge available to ordinary people is inference, knowledge that is
obtained through evidence and reason. The common inferential arguments for rebecoming trace back to
Dharmakīrti. His arguments are quite technical, and not many translations of his works exists, but I
was able to find a [paper](https://www.unm.edu/~rhayes/rebirth.pdf) that summarizes his arguments.
In the interest of brevity, I will only cover his first argument. The rest are rebuttals to common
objections.

Dharmakīrti's first argument is based on analyzing the cause of the current moment of consciousness.
The opponents to rebirth, the Cārvākas already mentioned earlier, argued that the current moment of
consciousness is caused only by the body, which is made up of purely physical aggregates. At death,
when those aggregates cease, so does the mind and the current moment of consciousness. From this
follows the views that the Buddha explicitly rejected as wrong view: no afterlife, no fruit or
result of good and bad deeds, and no beings that are reborn spontaneously.

Dharmakīrti argues that every effect must have a cause that is of the same class as itself. The mind
is luminous, non-physical, subjective, and cognizant. Matter on the other hand is none of these
things, but their very opposite. Therefore, the present moment of consciousness can only have as its
cause another moment of consciousness. When that logic is applied to the first moment of a single
life, it follows that there must have been a previous moment of consciousness that caused it or that
it has no cause at all. When applied to the last moment of consciousness of a single life, it
follows that there must be a next moment of consciousness that it causes or that it has no effect at
all. Since all moments of consciousness we experience have a cause and an effect, it follows that
the stream of consciousness precedes and follows this current life, even if the argument cannot
explain the mechanism by which this happens.

The rest of his arguments are rebuttals to objections that arise from observing the intimate
relationship between the mind and the body, and taking it as evidence that the mind is a product of
the body. These counterarguments live to this day, where the incredible advances in science are used
as evidence for the exact position that modernist interpreters told us was too advanced for the time
of the Buddha.

I believe it's unnecessary to go into the details of these rebuttals because they ignore that
Buddhism never denies that the physical aggregates of the body condition the mind. It only denies
that the body causes the mind, much the same way a computer conditions the flow of electricity
without ever causing it. All current scientific evidence is perfectly compatible with both
positions, so only direct proof of a moment of consciousness arising out of matter would prove
materialism.

The Buddhist position only requires that the present moment of consciousness is caused by a previous
moment of consciousness, something that is observable to all of us every day. Those who advance a
position in which our subjective, cognizant, and luminous experience arises out of something that
possesses none of those qualities must be the ones shouldering the burden of proof.

Dharmakīrti's argument is not meant to be a proof of rebecoming on its own, but a proof that a
continuity of consciousness beyond birth and death is not only possible, but much more plausible
than the alternative. Those who wish to verify that rebecoming works through dependent origination
must undertake the path of direct perception.

### Direct Perception

The main source of knowledge considered valid for verifying rebecoming is direct perception. As
mentioned by Maitreya in the Rice Seedling Sutra, seeing dependent origination is seeing the Dharma,
so the entire path of Buddhism consists of seeing and reversing the causes and conditions that lead
to the cycle of rebecoming. For both the paths of arahat and Bodhisattva, the initial stage of
awakening that establishes one firmly on the path brings about a direct perception of the lack of a
personal self or the emptiness of all phenomena respectively. Such direct perception dispels any
doubts about the mechanism of rebecoming. Such direct seeing clarifies the mechanism of rebecoming
that inference alone cannot reveal. It should be noted that direct perception about rebecoming could
happen even earlier, but that these stages are the traditional milestones at which such confidence
on rebecoming occurs at the latest.

Apart from continuing on the path until reaching either stream-entry or the first Bodhisattva bhūmi,
there are practices meant to provide access to memories of past lives, considered one of the three
supernormal knowledges. The Buddha gives a summary of the instructions in [MN
36](https://suttacentral.net/mn36/en/sujato):

> With the giving up of pleasure and pain, and the ending of former happiness and sadness, I entered
> and remained in the fourth absorption, without pleasure or pain, with pure equanimity and
> mindfulness. But even such pleasant feeling did not occupy my mind.
>
> When my mind had immersed in samādhi like this—purified, bright, flawless, rid of corruptions,
> pliable, workable, steady, and imperturbable—I extended it toward recollection of past lives. I
> recollected my many kinds of past lives, with features and details.

One who wishes to undertake this practice should find a qualified teacher for detailed instructions,
but the gist of the practice can be understood from this excerpt. The meditator must first learn to
access the fourth jhāna, come out, and direct their mind towards recollecting earlier and earlier
moments of their current life. After reaching the threshold of their birth, the purified and steady
mind cultivated by the fourth jhāna allows them to recollect the earlier moment of consciousness and
so on. The practice mirrors Dharmakīrti's argument for the continuity of consciousness, but instead
of using inference, it uses the fourth jhāna as an instrument that allows to make the same
observation directly. It is important to note that the jhāna here is a state of deep absorption, not
the fourth jhāna of "lite" jhāna systems that have become recently popular. Those interested on
learning why the lite jhānas cannot be used for this practice can read my
[essay](@/essays/aural-jhana-guide.md) on the subject.

Buddhist vehicles also contain practices meant to clear away the accumulated karma of past lives
without needing to recollect them. Although they do not reveal past live memories directly, the
effects of these practices reveal that the habitual traces of a sentient being are too large to
belong to a single lifetime. From personal experience and without revealing too much due to the vows
to secrecy in Vajrayana, the most powerful of these practices in the Vajrayana tradition are too
simple, too strong (even in very small doses), and produce results consistent with the intended
effects (strange dreams involving almost forgotten memories, emotional releases, etc.) that make
rebecoming much easier to accept without direct knowledge of past lives.

## Conclusion

The Buddha's teachings on rebecoming are clear and consistent across all Buddhist traditions. His
teachings on dependent origination take the observable mechanism of causation and expand on it to
provide a diagnosis and a cure for the suffering of sentient beings. Beyond the extremes of
believing consciousness disappears at death or that a permanent self transmigrates from life to
life, the Buddha's middle way remains the thread that holds all 84,000 Buddhist teachings together.
Like Maitreya explained to Śāriputra, seeing dependent origination is seeing the Dharma, and seeing
the Dharma is seeing the Buddha. In contrast, movements and teachers that deny rebecoming or teach
that it only applies to this life will continue to blind their followers to the Dharma.
