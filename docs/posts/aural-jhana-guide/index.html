    An Aural Lineage Guide to the Jhānas

    ← Back to blog section

    Due to the length of this guide, a table of contents with short summaries of each section is provided below. A reader interested in learning what jhānas are and how to practice them can read the sections Defining the Jhānas and Instructions on Dhyāna. The other sections provide a comparative analysis of the definitions of the jhānas across living Buddhist traditions and across several modernist interpretations.

    • Introduction: Defining Buddhism as an aural tradition that is transmitted by hearing the instructions from one's teacher, and how traditions that adhere to an aural understanding of the jhānas have a remarkably consistent understanding of them.
    • Defining the Jhānas: A detailed definition of the jhānas based on the Abhidharmakośa, a text that is widely studied across East Asian and Tibetan Buddhist and that has been transmitted in an unbroken aural lineage.
    • Jhānas Across Buddhist Vehicles: A comparative analysis of the definitions of the jhānas across Theravada, Mahayana, and Vajrayana traditions. Special attention is given to how they define the first jhāna in a manner consistent with the view and goals of their vehicle.
    • Modernist Reinterpretations of the Jhānas: A comparative analysis of the definitions of the jhānas across several modernist interpretations that are not based on an aural lineage. The contradictions between these interpretations and the traditional understanding of the jhānas are highlighted.
    • Instructions on Dhyāna: Instructions on how to practice the jhānas based on the understanding presented in earlier sections, highlighting how the practice can be adapted for lay practitioners without watering down the traditional thresholds.

    Introduction🔗

    "Thus have I heard ...". Many sutras and tantras in the Buddhist canon begin with this formula or a variation of it, called a nidāna. Far from being a mere literary device, the nidāna hints at the correct mode of receiving Buddhist teachings, which is done through listening to the teachings from a qualified teacher, who in turn received them in the same way from their teacher.

    The teacher transmits not only the words of the teachings, but also their meanings, which cannot be fully captured in the words, as the texts often omit core details that are only known to those who received the aural teachings that come with the texts. Even when the meanings are straightforward, they can be lost when the words are translated from their original languages and as the meanings in those original languages change over centuries.

    Attempting to understand Buddhist teachings through reading alone is a modern phenomenon that assumes the texts can be read and understood in isolation from the aural lineage that preserved them. Often, this assumption comes from cultures where this mode of reading religious texts reflects the dominant Protestant Christian culture and history. Without a valid aural lineage, all rereading of the teachings are reinterpretations that can easily lead to misunderstandings. Even if such a reinterpretation happens to be correct, we can only assert so by comparing it to an existing aural lineage.

    Outsiders to the Buddhist tradition might think this is a minor problem or that resorting to claims of lineage boils down to a mere appeal to authority. Fundamentally, the problem is that without the instructions that are only transmitted aurally from teacher to student, it is impossible to truly know if one has properly understood the view and methods of a tradition. From a Buddhist standpoint, lineages with only textual transmission and no aural lineage have been forever lost and cannot be reestablished. When the last living aural lineage dies out, Buddhism itself is lost in a world system until a new Buddha arises and turns the wheel of Dharma.

    The problems with trying to turn Buddhism into a textual tradition become evident when looking at the jhānas, a sequence of meditative states described in all Buddhist traditions which has recently seen a surge in popularity, even outside Buddhist circles. Those looking for instruction on the jhānas will quickly find themselves lost in a sea of conflicting interpretations, all of which claim to be either the real teachings or use the term jhāna to give the impression of being based on ancient transmission instead of modern fabrications.

    In reality, as this guide will extensively document, the understanding of the jhānas across living Buddhist traditions is remarkably consistent. This does not mean that there are no differences, but they are mostly constrained to changes in the thresholds and definitions of the mental factors that define the first jhāna. These changes were done to match the view and goals of the vehicle, not as free-form reinterpretations done to lower the bar for attaining the first jhāna. These differences are too nuanced to fit into a simple binary of "light" vs "heavy" jhānas. Elements of agreement across living aural lineages include:

    • The definition of a jhāna as a meditative state of concentration characterized by the presence of certain mental factors and the absence of certain hindrances.
    • The definition of the rūpa and arūpa jhānas as a meditative sequence (which is what is commonly referred to as "the jhānas").
    • The factors that define the first jhāna and defining the higher jhānas as states derived by progressively dropping them. Higher jhānas are not defined by higher states of bliss but by higher unification of mind that allows some existing factors to drop out naturally.
    • The definition of the arūpa jhānas as produced by concentrating on progressively more subtle formless mental objects (hence the name "formless" jhānas) within the purified state of concentration established in the fourth jhāna.
    • The higher jhānas, by definition, preclude any notion that there can be any valid "light" versions of them as they are by definition states of absorption so deep that the mental factors that allow for attending to new mental objects are entirely absent.
    • The understanding of the jhānas as both states of concentration and realms of existence in the form and formless deva realms. Therefore, the jhānas cannot be anything but strong experiences that change the aggregates of the practitioner to better match the extraordinary experience of the beings in those realms.

    By contrast, modern interpretations of the jhānas that are not based on an aural lineage differ wildly from each other and from traditional definitions. As meditation had become out of fashion in Theravada countries and many (if not all) meditative aural lineages died out, the revival of meditation in the 19th and 20th centuries was done without the benefit of a living aural lineage, leading to a wide variety of interpretations within Theravada itself. The situation of systems that attempt to divorce the jhānas from their Buddhist context is even more diverse.

    Defining the Jhānas🔗

    This section will define how the jhānas are defined in a living aural lineage that has no documented break and revival in its transmission, the Abhidharma tradition which has been preserved in Chinese and Indo-Tibetan aural lineages. Specifically, the definitions will be pulled from Vasubandhu's Abhidharmakośa, as it is a widely studied text across East Asian and Tibetan Buddhist traditions. My own training has been in the Dzogchen tradition, whose understanding of many topics is based on Abhidharma. The Abhidharmakośa is the text that my own teachers use when expounding on those topics. As will be seen later, understanding jhānas is not nearly as complicated as it appears when one does not have the benefit of proper instruction.

    The prevalence of notions about textual purity and the primacy of earlier texts over later commentaries in modernist interpretations of the jhānas requires that I clarify the true purpose of commentaries within an aural lineage and why they are more, not less, valid than interpretations of sutras that ignore them in favor of textual archeology devoid of an aural lineage.

    As I pointed out in the introduction, Buddhism is not a textual tradition, but an aural one. When a teacher transmits the teachings to a student, they transmit not only the words of the text, but meanings that are often not supplied by the words and were left implicit in the texts. From its beginning, receiving Buddhist teachings has involved going to such a teacher and listening to their words. When the community of monastics and scholars grew, they began to write down and systematize the teachings in a way that is not much different from modern attempts. The difference, however, is that those commentaries were written by people who had the knowledge of the aural lineage and thus were able to fill in the implicit meanings not found in the texts.

    Differences in aural traditions and their commentaries are differences in the view and goals of their vehicle and in how the lineage of practitioners interpreted the teachings and expanded their understanding. Assigning a negative value to this process of interpretation and expansion within an aural lineage is completely foreign to the Buddhist tradition and closely aligns with views of "original" religious texts as sola scriptura that come from Protestant theology. This guide will make no further apologies for relying on living aural traditions over outsider interpretations that ignore them.

    With that out of the way, let us look at how the dhyānas (the Sanskrit term for jhāna) are defined in chapter 8 of the Abhidharmakośa. Quotes will not be supplied as the text is a commentary on Vasubandhu's own verses, which are often interspersed with commentary, debate, and counter-arguments, making for sentences that are not easy to quote verbatim.

    The text starts by asserting that there are four dhyānas and that each of them has two aspects, as a concentration state and as an existence. This highlights something that is often misunderstood or left out of modern interpretations. Dhyānas are not just meditative states, but also map to realms of existence that sentient beings can be reborn into. The concentration states are meant to replicate the experience of living in those realms. Traditional warnings about jhānas often center around their potential for causing rebirth in them, something not seen as a positive for practitioners of the path that aims for ending rebirth.

    Dhyāna as an absorption is then defined as the application of the mind on a single object, for all dhyānas have samādhi (concentration or one-pointedness) as their nature. The term ekāgratā is a synonym of samādhi and its Pali equivalent ekaggatā is often listed as a factor of the first jhāna. It is important to note that when Vasubandhu omits samādhi or ekāgratā from the list of factors of the first jhāna, it is because it is already implied from this definition. Formulations of the jhānas in the Pali Canon also omit this factor for the same reason. Some modern interpreters have taken this omission to mean that it is not a factor, another example of how the aural lineage is necessary to understand the teachings.

    The text then asserts that samādhi is a mental factor that all beings possess but that it is weak and unstable in ordinary beings. The dhyānas are states where samādhi is strong and stable. However, not all states of concentration are dhyānas, only those states that possess excellent qualities.

    Now we are ready to define the factors of the first dhyāna. The text defines them as vicāra, prīti, and sukha. Readers familiar with definitions of the first jhāna might be surprised to find only three factors instead of the usual five. There is no contradiction here, as the text also asserts that the mental factor of vitarka is present implicitly in vicāra just like smoke accompanies fire. As explained above, the presence of samādhi is also implicit. The text does not define these mental factors in this section because they were already defined in the other chapters. The definitions of these factors are as follows:

    • Vitarka: the initial application of the mind to the object of meditation.
    • Vicāra: the sustained application of the mind to the object of meditation. Since sustaining the mind on an object implies an initial placement, vitarka is only implicit in this definition of the first dhyāna.
    • Prīti: a pleasurable, joyful, and uplifting feeling that arises from continuously placing the mind on the object of meditation.
    • Sukha: a pleasant and easy feeling that arises in the body-mind continuum from the stability of the mind on the object of meditation. It is meant to be a more subtle and refined feeling than the pleasure of prīti.
    • Samādhi: the neutral mental factor of one-pointedness or concentration that supports and unifies all the other factors. Because it is implicit and supports all the other factors, it was not listed explicitly.

    A common criticism of Abhidharma is to paint it as a scholastic tradition that overcomplicates the teachings and has no practical value. However, Vasubandhu's definition of the first dhyāna is by itself a practical guide to reaching the first dhyāna. Even the very order of the factors is not accidental. So what are the instructions? One picks a wholesome object of meditation (such as the breath, a mantra, or a visualized image) and applies and sustains the mind on it. Due to the wholesomeness of the object, the meditator experiences uplifting joy and pleasure. This pleasure then infuses the body-mind continuum with a pleasant and easy feeling. When all these factors are present and unified by samādhi, the meditator has achieved the first dhyāna.

    As will be explained later, variations of the first dhyāna across different Buddhist vehicles are due to the different views and goals of those vehicles and how they interpret the thresholds of those mental factors that count as a valid first dhyāna. However, the core definition and this sequence of practice does not change across them.

    The second, third, and fourth dhyānas are characterized by the dropping out of the factors of vicāra (and vitarka by implication), prīti, and then sukha respectively. Equanimity is not listed here as an explicit factor of the fourth dhyāna, but it is implicit that once the factors of mental and bodily pleasure and ease drop out, the mind is sustained without bias on the object of meditation.

    From this definition there are several important points to note. First, higher dhyānas are not reached by trying to forcefully drop them out or by trying to induce higher states of prīti or sukha. They are reached when the factors needed to reach the next dhyāna become strong and unified enough that the remaining factor drops out naturally.

    Second and more important to the discussion of "light" vs "heavy" jhānas, it is central to understand what a mental factor is, and what the dropping out of vicāra in the second dhyāna practically implies. Vasubandhu defines a mental factor as a mental state or capacity that arises in conjunction with a mind and is associated with it. When vicāra drops out in the second dhyāna, it simply means that a mind in that state does not have the capacity to initiate and sustain attention on mental objects, other than the object of meditation in which attention is currently absorbed.

    This is a crucial and basic point that is missed in modern interpretations of the jhānas that advocate for a "light" version of the jhānas. While a "light" version of the first dhyāna simply means to change the threshold at which the factors of the first dhyāna are considered present, a "light" version of the second is incoherent by definition. A state of concentration so deep that the mind cannot attend to mental objects means that the meditator cannot even form the intention to get out of that state. It is for this reason that the instructions for mastering the higher dhyānas include mastering the ability to set a determination to exit at a certain time. Without this determination, the meditator will remain in the higher dhyāna until the mental factors subside on their own or the meditator is interrupted, moved around, etc. There is nothing light about such a state. Even if vicāra is understood as gross thinking as some interpreters do, it would still be impossible to have the thought "I need to stop my meditation session" so the same problem would arise.

    The text continues to define the arūpa dhyānas as states that possess four skandhas. To understand what this means we have to remember that the dhyānas are also realms of existence, so an understanding of the deva realms is necessary. A deva is a sentient being that is reborn in an extremely pleasant realm of existence as a result of the ripening of good karma. There are three types of deva realms where devas enjoy progressively more subtle and refined pleasures: desire, form, and formless devas.

    Desire devas are the easiest to understand, since human beings are also born in the desire realm. In this realm, beings are concerned with satisfying their desire for sensual pleasures and avoiding sensual pain. Devas are born in places where suffering is minimal and pleasure is constant, beyond what could be imagined in the human realm. They have beautiful bodies, live in beautiful palaces, possess extra sensory powers, have very long lifespans, and they can spend it all engaging in sensual pleasures.

    Form devas have transcended the desire realm and take pleasure in the refined pleasures of the mind that are very similar to the pleasures of the jhānas. They however, still have a body, that is, a rūpa skandha. Formless devas have transcended the form realm and take pleasure in even more refined mental pleasures and as a result they do not have a body nor sense organs. However refined any of the deva realms might be, they are still subject to impermanence, so one should not have the goal of being reborn in them.

    The text then defines the associated concentration states of the arūpa dhyānas as arising out of separation from lower spheres. It defines separation as the process by which the meditator turns away from the pleasures of the current dhyāna towards an even more refined object. The arūpa dhyānas are defined of two types, the first three of which are named after what their preparatory exercise is based on. The final one is based on the weakness of perception, neither being active in the ordinary way nor completely absent.

    • Ākāśānantyāyatana: base of infinite space.
    • Vijñānānantyāyatana: base of infinite consciousness.
    • Ākiñcanyāyatana: base of nothingness.
    • Naivasaṃjñānāsaṃjñāyatana: base of neither-perception-nor-non-perception.

    Because dhyānas are also realms of existence, the meditator is transforming their aggregates into the corresponding refined states of existence. Any idea that they are reached by trying to induce higher states of bliss or by thinking of space or nothingness within a state of light concentration is not at all consistent with the understanding of dhyānas as concentration states that replicate the experiences of the form and formless deva realms. Only the fourth jhāna, where the grosser mental factors of prīti and sukha have dropped out, can be the proper basis for practicing the arūpa dhyānas. Although this is not mentioned explicitly in the Abhidharmakośa, Theravada meditation manuals make this point very clear.

    Later in the chapter, Vasubandhu defines sāmantakas, prefatory or threshold absorptions through which one enters the basic absorptions. Each basic absorption has such a threshold state. In a sāmantaka, the meditator is not yet established in the root absorption itself, but is detaching from the lower state that must be abandoned in order to enter it. As the next section explains, other Abhidharma traditions show their own versions of these threshold states. Their existence shows that aural traditions already have a way to talk about intermediate states of concentration that do not fully qualify as the first dhyāna, and that lowering the threshold for the first dhyāna risks confusing intermediate states for the real thing.

    Jhānas Across Buddhist Vehicles🔗

    Theravada🔗

    The treatment of Theravada jhānas requires special handling because the aural jhāna meditation lineages that should be the basis for comparison had mostly died out or fallen into obscurity by the 19th century. Existing meditative lineages were actively suppressed for being deemed too esoteric and not in line with the version of Buddhism that was being promoted, so finding information about their practice and about any surviving branches is more difficult. First we will have to look at the textual sources, taken as a whole without privileging suttas over Abhidhamma and commentaries, and then look at the mainstream modern languages that trace their lineages to those revival movements.

    Textual sources🔗

    Before going into how the jhānas are defined in the surviving textual sources, it is important to note that the jhānas in Theravada contain an additional ingredient that was not mentioned explicitly in the Abhidharmakośa definition. That ingredient is renunciation and sense restraint. This should not come as a surprise, for this vehicle is based on the ideal of the monastic that gives up attachment to worldly pleasures.

    Such renunciation and sense restraint is not optional and is seen as a necessary condition for entering the jhānas. It is not a coincidence that suttas that mention the jhānas often start with some variation of the formula: "Quite secluded from sensual pleasures, secluded from unwholesome states". Many modern interpreters of the jhānas think that this seclusion is a nice to have or that it has a much lower threshold than monastic renunciation, but the refusal to exercise the required sense restraint explains why their reinterpretations tend to lower the thresholds and why they deem traditional ones as impossible or unnecessary.

    As for the factors of the first jhāna, the Pali Canon lists five factors: vitakka, vicāra, pīti, sukha, and ekaggatā. Some suttas omit the last factor, but as we have seen from the Abhidharmakośa, jhānas are defined as states of ekaggatā, so this factor is implicit in every jhāna. Abhidhamma and meditation manuals like the Visuddhimagga are consistent in listing the same five factors.

    Just as in the Abhidharmakośa, the second, third, and fourth jhānas are defined by the dropping out of the factors of vitakka and vicāra, pīti, and sukha respectively. In suttas and Abhidhamma, jhānas are also defined as states of concentration and existence and the mapping of jhānas to the deva realms is also consistent. One thing to note is that the Buddha attained some arūpa jhānas through training with the ascetic teachers he met before his enlightenment (MN 26). However, this does not mean that the arūpa jhānas do not require high levels of concentration, as Abhidhamma also defines ekaggatā as a mental factor that is present in all beings. Hence, teachers of other traditions can also have access to refined states of concentration, even if they do not fully match the definition of the jhānas.

    The definition of vitakka and vicāra in the Pali Canon is often misunderstood as gross thinking, but Abhidhamma defines them as the initial and sustained application of the mind to the object of meditation just like the Abhidharmakośa. That means the point about the second jhāna being a state of incredibly deep absorption by its very definition also applies to the Theravada jhānas.

    The Visuddhimagga contains more explicit instructions for the higher jhānas than found in Mahayana because those traditions do not practice them as part of the standard path. Here the manual is consistent with the Abhidharmakośa in defining the factors of the first jhāna. One of the main points of contention in discussions about "light" vs "heavy" jhānas is that this manual sets the threshold for the first jhāna at not only the presence of the five factors and absence of the five hindrances, but to the attainment of full absorption. Most modern Theravada traditions either respect this threshold or ignore the sequence of jhānas altogether as not required for vipassanā practice.

    The common critique from the proponents of "light" jhānas is that this threshold is too high and that lay practitioners benefit from a more relaxed definition of the jhānas. However, Abhidhamma already defines a precursor absorption state called upacāra samādhi much in the same way as the Abhidharmakośa defines sāmantakas. If the criteria are too light, then it might cross the threshold into upacāra samādhi, and the resulting state would not be a jhāna despite any benefits it might bring.

    While their arguments might have some merit when talking about the first jhāna, they are completely incoherent when talking about the second and higher jhānas. The high threshold for the first jhāna in this commentary makes complete sense given that the goal of the Visuddhimagga is to help practitioners reach mastery of all the jhānas, not to treat the first jhāna as a goal in itself.

    The Visuddhimagga contains instructions for mastering going into and out of the jhānas, which explicitly mention the requirement of setting a determination to exit the jhāna at a certain time. It also contains instructions for the arūpa jhānas, which are all based on achieving the purified state of concentration of the fourth jhāna and then applying it to meditating on the corresponding formless objects with the aid of preparatory exercises. Mastery of the fourth jhāna is also a prerequisite for developing the higher knowledges (abhiññā), abilities like knowing one's past lives, divine eye, and reading minds. The suttas also make it clear that the Buddha attained these higher knowledges through mastery of the jhānas, so interpretations of the fourth jhāna that cannot serve as a basis for developing these higher knowledges are not consistent with the traditional understanding of the jhānas, even if only the suttas are considered as valid.

    Finally, the surviving textual sources of the Theravada tradition also define the jhānas as states of existence that match the experiences of the form and formless deva realms. The suttas themselves explain the relationship and clearly articulate that the sublime existence of the beings living in those realms. Form and formless devas are beings living extraordinarily long lives spent entirely basking in the refined experiences of their concentration. Furthermore, formless devas are born without a body and without sense organs. These descriptions are never meant to be taken metaphorically and put a huge dent to the idea that the higher jhānas are light states of concentration compatible with walking around, talking, and even moving one's attention to a different mental object.

    Having looked at the surviving textual sources, we can see that the definition of the jhānas is highly consistent with the Abhidharmakośa definition when both Abhidhamma and commentaries are considered as valid, which was the case when the aural lineages of the day put those teachings in writing. Now we can look at the modern Theravada lineages and see how they compare to the combined textual sources.

    Pa-Auk lineage🔗

    This lineage is based on the teachings of the Burmese meditation master Pa-Auk Sayadaw, who took the approach of following the Visuddhimagga. Therefore, there is no surprise that the definition of the jhānas is consistent with the view presented in the last section.

    Thai Forest Tradition🔗

    This tradition tends to have greater variation in how the jhānas are defined across different teachers because it is not based on a single meditation manual or lineage, but on a movement of Thai monastics that left to practice in the forests with the aim of reviving meditation practice. Some important monastics like Ajahn Brahm hold a threshold for the first jhāna that is consistent with the Visuddhimagga.

    Others like Ṭhānissaro Bhikkhu argue for a lower threshold of absorption that still allows for bodily sensations, because otherwise investigation and insight become impossible. Mahayana uses similar justifications as will be explained in the next section. By contrast, the Visuddhimagga and Pa-Auk lineages use the absorption of the jhānas to cultivate a stable and unified mind that can be used to investigate phenomena after coming out of the jhāna. So even on this point, the difference is not categorical but a matter of pedagogy.

    Some presentations in this tradition differ in how they define the factors of vitakka and vicāra, with most agreeing that they refer to the initial and sustained application of the mind to an object and some thinking that they refer to thinking and pondering. Still, all agree that these mental factors drop out in the second jhāna, and I was not able to find any presentation that implies either attention, thinking, and all the activities associated with them can be present in the second jhāna and above.

    Mahasi Insight Tradition🔗

    The Mahasi tradition is based on the teachings of the Burmese meditation master Mahasi Sayadaw, who took a very different approach to the jhānas than Pa-Auk and the Thai Forest Tradition. Rather than trying to reconstruct the jhānas following the Visuddhimagga or the suttas, Mahasi Sayadaw sees them as states of absorption that are not necessary for the main approach of his system. This approach is called vipassanā, which centers around noticing the arising and passing away of phenomena in the present moment and discovering the three marks of existence (impermanence, suffering, and non-self) in them. His system includes a separate map of states and stages of insight that are meant to be reached through vipassanā practice, but there is no claim that these stages are equivalent to the jhānas.

    Goenka Vipassanā Tradition🔗

    The Goenka tradition is based on the teachings of the Burmese lay meditation teacher S. N. Goenka. Like the Mahasi tradition, it is a modern Burmese vipassanā lineage rather than a jhāna lineage. Its practice emphasizes morality, concentration, systematic observation of bodily sensations, and insight into impermanence. While concentration is an important prerequisite in the system, the sequence of rūpa and arūpa jhānas is not taught as the standard path of practice, nor are the insight stages of the system presented as equivalent to the jhānas.

    Mahayana🔗

    Mahayana reframes the understanding of the dhyānas by focusing on the dual cultivation of śamatha and vipaśyanā. Śamatha means the cultivation of calm and concentration and is associated with cultivating the factors associated with the first dhyāna. Vipaśyanā means the cultivation of insight into the emptiness of phenomena.

    To understand why only the development of the first dhyāna is emphasized at the early stages of the path and why it is paired and integrated with vipaśyanā, it is necessary to understand Mahayana's critique of the path of practitioners that aim to liberate only themselves. Mahayana defines two obscurations that prevent beings from attaining complete enlightenment: afflictive and cognitive obscurations. The former is the affliction that causes repeated rebirth in samsāra. The latter is the affliction that prevents beings from fully realizing that all phenomena are empty of inherent existence and thus prevents them from attaining complete enlightenment.

    According to Mahayana, arahats have fully eradicated the afflictive obscurations but still have cognitive obscurations because they have only realized the emptiness of self and not the emptiness of phenomena. Because of this, arahats believe that the path of a Bodhisattva is optional and rest in nirvāna until they are roused from it by a Buddha who inspires them to take the Bodhisattva vow and clear away their remaining cognitive obscurations.

    Since Bodhisattvas are already motivated to clear away their cognitive obscurations, their development centers around understanding the emptiness of phenomena. The cultivation of śamatha is meant to produce a stable and unified mind that can then be used to investigate phenomena and realize their emptiness. Given that the second dhyāna is a state of absorption where the mind cannot attend to mental objects, higher dhyānas cannot be unified with vipaśyanā. The first dhyāna, on the other hand, must be defined in a way that allows for mental monitoring and investigation of phenomena, which is why the threshold for one-pointedness is not so high that it precludes such investigation and sensory input.

    The Mahayana defines ten stages in the path of a Bodhisattva who has realized the emptiness of phenomena, called the bhūmis. Traditional sources map the development of the start of the practice of the higher dhyānas to start in the third bhūmi and to end in the fifth at the latest. However, these stages can be reached by cultivating no more than the first dhyāna, and they occur very late in the path, making the higher dhyānas completely optional for the vast majority of practitioners. Given that Mahayana is also a path of renunciation, the same point about the need for renunciation and sense restraint from Theravada applies here.

    The standard presentation of the dual development of śamatha and vipaśyanā in Mahayana is presented in Kamalaśīla's Bhāvanākrama ("Stages of Meditation"). Its framework of the nine stages of mental abiding depicts the meditator as a monk pursuing an elephant led by a monkey. The elephant represents the initially wild mind and the monkey represents distraction and mental agitation. A rabbit representing subtle dullness appears later on the elephant's back. Through the sustained application of mindfulness and clear comprehension, the monkey is subdued, the elephant gradually whitens, and the mind eventually rests without effort on its object. The images summarize the stages of development of the first dhyāna, which produces a pliable and unified mind ready for the cultivation of vipaśyanā and the realization of emptiness.

    Vajrayana🔗

    Vajrayana is a division of Mahayana that contains uncommon methods that are meant to accelerate the path and allow practitioners to reach enlightenment in a single lifetime (in the best case) instead of the countless lifetimes that are usually required in the common Mahayana path. While the methods of Vajrayana are numbered in the hundreds, they all have in common three main features:

    • They are gated behind initiation (abhiṣeka) from a qualified teacher and require students to take up vows (samaya) to practice them. The relationship between the teacher and student is of utmost importance in Vajrayana and separates its methods from methods of common Mahayana that are esoteric but not gated behind initiation and vows.
    • They are based on a view of the human body that is not present in common Mahayana, centered around features of the human subtle anatomy. It is thanks to this understanding that the methods of Vajrayana can accelerate the path, allowing practitioners much earlier access to more refined samādhis.
    • They are not based on a view of renunciation and sense restraint, but instead on a view based on transforming sense objects into useful supports for the path.

    Another categorization of Vajrayana methods is to group them into the stages of creation and completion. In the creation stage, practitioners visualize themselves as a deity and their surroundings as the pure land of that deity. In the completion stage, practitioners work with the subtle anatomy of their body to transcend the visualized forms of the creation stage and directly access the nature of mind.

    Vajrayana views the development of the first dhyāna very similarly to Mahayana, but the meditation object is changed to special objects that allow for samādhi to develop more quickly (such as a visualized deity, a sadhana, a mantra, or a visualization of subtle anatomy). Focusing on these objects during practice naturally develops the factors of the first dhyāna, but at a pace impossible to achieve through the methods of common Mahayana alone.

    Mahamudra and Dzogchen🔗

    Mahamudra and Dzogchen are two closely related systems of practice that are often grouped together as a special division within Vajrayana because they work by introducing students directly to the nature of mind and then having them take it as the path after (or instead of) training in the creation and completion stages.

    The main way in which these two vehicles reinterpret the first dhyāna is by redefining the factor of one-pointedness. Mahamudra and Dzogchen are not paths of renunciation because they do not view sense objects as impure and in need of being abandoned. Neither are they paths of transformation, since the stages of creation and completion are to be abandoned after a certain point. Instead, they are paths of self-liberation in which all phenomena that appear to the mind are to be seen as merely the play of the mind. Therefore, the factor of one-pointedness is redefined as all six senses directly meeting their objects without distraction, aversion, or attachment.

    Another reason why one-pointedness is redefined in this way is that the first dhyāna is only a preliminary for practicing the main practices of Mahamudra and Dzogchen. These main practices are to be done with all the senses open and allow for the discovery of what Dzogchen calls natural concentration. Once the practitioner ascertains the nature of mind based on the instructions of their teacher, they discover that all phenomena appear to that mind as reflections in a mirror, without ever straying from it. Given that straying is impossible, one-pointedness is discovered to be a natural factor of the mind that requires no effort to develop once it is discovered.

    Dzogchen texts are very clear that this natural concentration lies outside the sequence of the dhyānas and of any concentration state that is based on putting effort into sustaining attention on an object. If the concentration of the dhyānas is like a swimmer learning finer and finer methods to stay afloat, natural concentration is like a swimmer discovering they are naturally buoyant and can float without any effort. As Longchenpa, the greatest scholar of this tradition, explains when sarcastically comparing the approach of Dzogchen to those of "great meditators", the two swimmers might look the same from the outside, but there is greater difference between them than between the earth and the sky.

    While the first dhyāna of these vehicles is not as absorbed as the first dhyāna of Theravada, it is much more flexible by allowing the practitioner to experience all sense objects without distraction. It is therefore a much more suitable basis for lay practitioners instead of attempting to redefine vehicles based on renunciation and sense restraint to fit their needs.

    Modernist Reinterpretations of the Jhānas🔗

    In contrast to how the jhānas are defined consistently across the different Buddhist vehicles, with only changes in the thresholds of the factors or re-definitions that fit the view of the vehicle, modernist interpretations of the jhānas usually commit some of the following errors in their attempts to interpret the jhānas in the absence of a living aural lineage. The most common ones include:

    • Ignorance of the universal definition of a jhāna as a meditative state defined by the presence of certain factors and the absence of certain hindrances, leading to definitions of the jhānas that are more about the emotional tone of the meditation experience than the presence or absence of specific factors.
    • Ignorance of what a mental factor is and what the complete absence of a mental factor implies. Specifically, the absence of the mental factors that allow for attending to mental objects implies the complete absorption of the mind in the object of meditation, and precludes the meditator from any kind of mental monitoring, or decision-making while in the authentic higher jhānas. This causes such interpreters to believe a "light" version of the higher jhānas is possible, while in reality it is not only impossible, but completely incoherent.
    • Downplaying traditional warnings about the dangers of engaging in the jhānas without cultivating the necessary ethical and wisdom qualities. While the traditional warnings often center around the potential for rebirth in form and formless deva realms, even a secular interpretation of these warnings would recognize the potential for misusing the jhānas for escapism, spiritual pride, or other unwholesome tendencies.
    • Taking jhānas from their traditional context, where they are part of an integrated system of practice based on sense restraint and renunciation, and instead treating them as a standalone practice that can be used to induce incredible states of bliss and sensual pleasure.
    • Admitting that their states are not the sequence of jhānas described in the Buddhist canon while still claiming the name and attempting to present them as derived from the Buddhist tradition.
    • Mistaking the arūpa jhānas as simply remaining in a light state of concentration while attending to a meditation on space, infinity, or nothingness and thinking that the change in tone of the meditation experience is the switch to a new jhāna.
    • Claiming that the sustainability of their versions of jhānas is evidence of their authenticity or value and that the difficulty of the authentic jhānas is evidence of the need to reinterpret them as lighter states.
    • Claiming that their definitions of the jhānas and the path are more consistent with the Buddha's original teachings with no evidence other than textual reconstruction based on the texts they consider valid. This is sometimes done by people reading those texts in translation without the benefits of knowing the source language.
    • Retrofitting the jhānas and the path to fit their own meditative experiences instead of accepting that those experiences do not match the traditional definitions. Often, this is done in tandem with claiming that traditional attainments are unrealistic, religious myths, or simply impossible.

    The rest of this section reviews some of the most popular jhāna reinterpretations and rates them on the above criteria.

    Tranquil Wisdom Insight Meditation (TWIM)🔗

    TWIM is a modern meditation system developed by Bhante Vimalaramsi that is based on what he considers to be the correct interpretation of the suttas. Right away we are confronted with the problem of relying on a single textual source with no aural tradition to back it up. TWIM claims that the jhānas can all be accessed fairly easily by using the bramaviharas (loving-kindness, compassion, empathetic joy, and equanimity) as the object of meditation, and by using their method of relaxation which they summarize in the teaching of the 6Rs. The 6Rs are a method of working with distractions in meditation by recognizing them, releasing them, relaxing, re-smiling, returning to the object of meditation, and repeating the process.

    It should be quite obvious that this method of working with distractions is incompatible with any of the higher jhānas, as the method itself depends on the presence of vitakka and vicāra, as understood in the Abhidharmakośa and the textual Theravada tradition. TWIM resolves this obvious contradiction by claiming that vitakka and vicāra are factors that apply only to the meditation object and that reaching the second jhāna simply means that the meditator no longer has to recall a phrase to induce the feeling of loving-kindness, compassion, empathetic joy, or equanimity. Mental factors, however, are defined as factors that apply to the mind in general, not to specific objects.

    The 6R method can also hinder the development of the first jhāna, as it encourages the meditator to constantly monitor their meditation and to move their attention to different objects, instead of sustaining attention on a single object until the other factors appear on their own. While the method is not without merit as a preliminary or a way to calm the mind at the start of a meditation session, it requires too much active management for the state of unification that characterizes all the jhānas.

    For the arūpa jhānas, TWIM claims that they can be reached after the fourth jhāna by simply allowing the object of meditation to a feeling of compassion that has a spacious feeling. They mention that these jhānas correspond to the formless realms, but they miss the significance of that fact, with instructions that clearly indicate the feeling of having a body is still present, and that the 6Rs method can still be operated. While they are right about the formless realms being associated with the arūpa jhānas and being accessed from the fourth jhāna, their version cannot be the same as the traditional version because vitakka and vicāra are still present.

    The organization behind TWIM has also been criticized for inflating the progress of their students and having them believe they have reached a much higher jhāna than they actually have. The most famous case of such inflation is the case of Delson Armstrong, a student of Bhante Vimalaramsi who was claimed to have reached all the jhānas, claimed to have reached the state of an arahant (the conclusion of the Theravada path) and quickly rose through the ranks of the organization. Delson himself later recanted his claims in a public interview, but in doing so he attempted to rationalize having to renounce his previous claims by saying that the traditional fetter model used to define who is an arahant is unrealistic, a common tactic when reinterpretation clearly fails to match traditional definitions.

    Leigh Brasington's "Light" Jhānas🔗

    Leigh Brasington is a meditation teacher trained in the Theravada tradition who has been teaching a version of the jhānas that he calls "light" jhānas. When pressed about it, he admits that his "light" jhānas are more likely not what the Buddha taught and not exactly what his teacher Ayya Khema taught, but that they are still useful and valid states of concentration. While the honesty is appreciated, he still attempts to present them as derived from the Buddhist tradition, by calling them jhānas, naming the book explaining them "Right Concentration", and attempting to find support for them in the suttas.

    He attempts to legitimize his lighter criteria for jhāna attainments by claiming that the original definition of the first dhyāna did not include the factor of one-pointedness because it is not present in some suttas. When it is present, he waves that fact away by claiming such suttas are later additions. However, as was already explained, all jhānas are defined by nature to be states of one-pointedness, so the absence of the term in some suttas does not mean that the factor is not present. A state where the other four factors are present but one-pointedness is not would not be that different from a state of joy and bliss induced by drugs that have the user jumping from one pleasant object to another.

    As part of the same argument, he presents vitakka and vicāra as factors that only meant thinking, in clear contradiction to living aural lineages that understand them as the initial and sustained application of the mind to the object of meditation. As stated before, even if this definition is accepted, the absence of thinking in the second jhāna and above would still preclude the meditator from forming the intention to freely exit the state. This exact point was noted by a user of a forum who opened a thread raising this objection and presenting instances where Leigh Brasington claims thinking is still possible in the formless jhānas. Leigh himself appears on that discussion but never addresses the point, instead choosing to focus on the fact that the user is not a teacher and does not have any students and eventually leaving the discussion.

    An important difference between his system and how jhānas are presented in traditional lineages is the inversion of the relationship between pleasure and concentration. Instead of unifying the mind on an object and allowing for pīti and sukha to arise as a result of the unification of vitakka and vicāra, he gives his students the instruction to first cultivate a feeling of pleasure, using the resulting pleasure to create a feedback loop, and then placing the mind on the resulting states. The instructions for the higher jhānas center around refining the tone of the induced feelings to match the more refined flavors of the higher jhānas. While there is no issue with using a good intention as the object of meditation, creating such feedback loops is not an instruction found in any aural lineage and requires constant changes in the object of attention that actively work against the unification of the mind. Brasington himself admits that there is no support for this method in the suttas, justifying his own invention on the fact that the suttas do not contain detailed instructions. He completely ignores that such instructions do exist and have been preserved in aural lineages, the central thesis of this guide.

    Leigh Brasington also expresses views of rebirth that directly contradict the traditional understanding of the concept as literally meaning that beings are reborn into new bodies after death. Instead, he de-emphasizes the importance of lifetime-to-lifetime rebirth and quotes Stephen Batchelor, another modernizer, stating that rebirth is simply how the Buddha chose to express his ideas in the mythic language of his time. Given the direct connection between the jhānas and the states of existence connected to them, this view of rebirth is incompatible with the proper understanding presented in the previous sections. This connection is made clear in the suttas and is not merely a product of Abhidhamma and later commentaries.

    Rob Burbea🔗

    The Insight Meditation tradition is an influential meditation movement that is largely based on the teachings of the Mahasi tradition, but also includes teachings from other Theravada teachers. Since it is mostly based on the Mahasi traditions, it should not really be included in this section, but since it has become an umbrella organization for many meditation teachers and centers, some of those teachers have taught their own versions of the jhānas. Therefore, this section focuses on the teachings of Rob Burbea, an Insight Meditation teacher and longtime resident teacher at Gaia House, one of the major lay retreat centers in the Western vipassanā movement. It is unclear from what source Rob Burbea derived his understanding of the jhānas, but it appears to be his own synthesis rather than based on the understanding of the jhānas presented in previous sections.

    The clearest evidence of the errors in his presentation of the jhānas comes from a public talk on the second jhāna. In this talk, he incorrectly defines vitakka and vicāra as thinking and pondering. He explains that these factors drop in the second jhāna when presenting the sutta definitions but is clearly unaware of the actual implications of their absence because he goes on to claim that one can go for a walk in the second jhāna. As was already explained, such thing is not possible because going for a walk requires the ability to attend to one's path, make decisions about when to swerve, turn, or stop, and to monitor one's surroundings for potential dangers. All of these require the mental functions of vitakka and vicāra.

    What about the arūpa jhānas, which he also teaches? In a talk on the fifth jhāna (the common name for the first arūpa jhāna), he teaches that upon reaching the fourth jhāna, one can then think of ways to break the solidity of form and to then play with perceptions until the sense of infinitude opens up. He also mentions how one can practice it with eyes open, by staring at space and then letting the mind experience a sense of space.

    Right away, multiple issues arise with this presentation. First, the method of reaching the arūpa jhānas is presented as not requiring to be in any previous jhāna but as a result of merely finding the right way of looking. Second, the formless perceptions that are the base for the arūpa jhānas are presented as something that can be played with and manipulated, a mere perception of space that allows the mind to become more open while still remaining aware of the sky and one's surroundings. Once again the point that these jhānas are meant to replicate the experience of beings in the formless realms is completely ignored, as if formless realms devas can even be aware of their surroundings when they are lacking a body and all sense faculties.

    These two talks should present enough evidence that the jhānas taught by Rob Burbea lack any of the consistent definitions and markers that are present in traditional lineages and that form and formless jhānas are redefined to be states of light concentration where the tone of the object and experience changes to try to match the description of the jhānas in the suttas.

    Jhourney🔗

    Jhourney is a meditation startup that fuses a "light" jhāna framework mostly inherited from TWIM, Leigh Brasington, Rob Burbea and other teachers with a modern, secular, and technology-driven approach to meditation. Therefore, all the points made in those sections also apply to Jhourney. What Jhourney lacks in doctrinal innovation, it makes up for in its marketing department. Visitors of their site unfamiliar with the jhānas would be forgiven for thinking that they are a modern meditation system invented in Silicon Valley to help its workers deal with stress and burnout.

    Jhourney tackles the disagreements about the jhānas on their FAQ:

    Don’t people disagree on what defines a jhana?

    There is disagreement about what exactly constitutes a jhana, ranging from something available to any committed meditator to something that only one in a million people will experience in their lifetimes. We’re less interested in establishing a consensus definition, and more interested in a particular phenomenon: a growing group of people (we estimate a few thousand) appear to have learned to enter into extraordinarily pleasurable, non-addictive states that they often describe as “life-changing” with only a few dozen or a few hundred hours of practice. We don’t have an opinion on whether these should be called jhana-lite or not called jhana at all. We just want to make these states more accessible.

    Here they commit several of the errors listed at the beginning of this section. First, they admit ignorance of and complete lack of curiosity about the very stable understanding of jhānas that I presented in the previous sections. Second, they claim to not care about what the states they teach should be called while still claiming the name to leverage the association. Third, they claim that the states they teach are "non-addictive", ignoring the clear warnings about the dangers of using the jhānas for escapism and sensual pleasure. Without wisdom, the jhānas produce a very subtle addiction to the refined pleasures they produce, which result in rebirth in samsara, even if that version of samsara is highly pleasurable and advanced. Even a secular interpretation of these warnings would recognize that basking in pleasure only strengthens the mind's attachment to said pleasures and the potential for misusing states of deep pleasurable absorption for escapism, spiritual pride, or other unwholesome tendencies. Fourth, they focus on the emotional tone of the experience and the tone of the practice, clearly ignoring that the most pleasure one feels in the sequence appears in the first jhāna, and that the second and higher jhānas are defined by the dropping out of the factors that cause such pleasure.

    It should come as no surprise that the founders of this startup have received no traditional training, have no connection to any aural lineage, have no understanding of the traditional definitions of the jhāna, and do not have the necessary hours of practice that should make customers comfortable taking them as teachers and facilitators. The promise they make in the very title of their website, "jhāna in days, not decades", is only possible by redefining the jhānas to a much lower standard, not by providing a true innovation that allows practitioners to reach the authentic jhānas in a much shorter time frame. This slogan catches them again in the act of attempting to equate the states they teach with the authentic jhānas while claiming on the other hand that they do not care about names and definitions.

    Another answer from their FAQ is also worth mentioning:

    Do you teach Dharma?

    We’ve all been inspired by Buddhist teachings, and while the jhanas predate Buddhism, the Suttas provide excellent practice instructions for learning them. That said, we’re not interested in teaching morality or Buddhist metaphysics on our retreats. We’re focused on helping people have deep meditative experiences using whatever tools are most efficient for that goal. If you’re interested in a more traditional Buddhist retreat, we’ve worked with many teachers over the years that we’d be happy to refer you to.

    Here we find another issue, attempting to separate the jhānas from the rest of the path while using instruction and terminology from a path based on ethical discipline, wisdom, renunciation, and sense restraint. The jhānas they market cannot be the same jhānas described in the suttas because those jhānas have those other qualities as prerequisites.

    Someone interested in becoming a Jhourney customer despite these issues should be ready to spend at least $1,500 for an online retreat and much more for in-person retreats. This price, which is much higher than those set by qualified teachers from living aural lineages, is justified based on their rhetoric of attempting to "scale" the jhānas through technological advances. Early in the company's history, they were working on an EEG device that could detect when a practitioner is in a jhāna and provide feedback based on machine learning. They have since abandoned that project, and recent interviews no longer mention it. Their newest attempt to give the appearance of a technological edge is to create an AI assistant that can guide practitioners through the jhānas. Besides how predictably their evolution follows the latest trends in Silicon Valley, it should be clear by this point that any external interruption, no matter how accurate, will reengage the mental factors that need to be absent in the second jhāna and above.

    The high price is not based on actual expertise, connection to an aural lineage, basic curiosity about the richness of the tradition they are appropriating, or an actual innovation that allows practitioners to reach the authentic jhānas in a much shorter time frame. Such innovation would at least make the whole enterprise interesting despite all other issues. I encourage those interested in learning the jhānas to avoid Jhourney and instead learn from qualified teachers from a living aural lineage.

    Conclusion🔗

    The specific examples presented in this section are not meant to be an exhaustive list of all modern reinterpretations of the jhānas, but rather to illustrate the common patterns that they follow. It is no wonder that so much confusion exists about the jhānas when their most well-known popularizers are people with little to none traditional training and connection to a living aural lineage or people with such connection that are ignoring their past training in favor of their own systems. When said teachers gain popularity, they attract followers who are then trained in these systems, experience some states they believe to be genuine jhānas, and go on to teach them, creating a self-sustaining ecosystem in which the ignorance of one teacher becomes endorsed and given an appearance of legitimacy.

    So what are they teaching instead? Since I cannot read the minds of those who claim to get results with these systems, I must assume that their experiences are real, that the states are interesting, pleasurable, useful, and even transformative. However, they are at best versions of the first jhāna with variations in the affective tone and intensity of pīti and sukha that are being incorrectly mapped to the whole sequence of jhānas. Some might not even be reaching the threshold for the first jhāna and instead are reaching access concentration or an even lighter state of concentration. At worst, these are pleasant meditation states that are completely outside the traditional sequence of jhānas and are being marketed as such to leverage the association with the Buddhist tradition.

    There is no evidence I could find that any of these systems are teaching the full sequence of jhānas or even reaching the threshold of the second one, and no evidence that their creators noticed the very obvious contradictions between their definitions and how the absence of vitakka and vicāra precludes the possibility that their interpretations of these higher jhānas are correct.

    Little experience or evidence is needed for them to map those experiences to the jhānas, and the many contradictory definitions and understandings from aural lineages are dismissed as religious myths, unrealistic attainments, or later corruption from the original teachings that they have somehow uncovered. One example of this pattern is how the mapping of jhānas to the form and formless realms and its implications are discarded in favor of treating the jhānas as light states of concentration.

    Previous attempts to point out these contradictions are either ignored, waved away by claiming that those bringing them up care too much about definitions or concepts (a ridiculous argument in light of the deep and vast scholarly tradition within Buddhism), or dismissed as concerns not important in the face of the "life-changing" experiences that their systems produce. As Bhante Sujato already explained in his direct response to Leigh Brasington, those who do not care about concepts, definitions, and are only interested in the results of their practices can simply name them something else. However, the cachet of the name "jhāna" and its association with two and a half millennia of Buddhist practice is too much to give up in favor of naming them something like "Super Bliss States".

    Instructions on Dhyāna🔗

    The last section presents instructions on how to practice the jhānas. It might seem contradictory to give my own instructions after spending so much time arguing for the necessity of receiving aural instructions from a qualified teacher, but I have several reasons for doing so.

    First, people interested in learning the jhānas will attempt them before seeking out a teacher, and in doing so will come across material that is not only inaccurate but potentially harmful to their practice and their understanding of how the jhānas fit into the rest of the path. Therefore, I believe it is a matter of risk reduction to provide some initial instructions that are based on a more accurate understanding. All those who put them in practice should seek out a qualified teacher when they are ready. If they find the instructions helpful and are interested in seeking a similar approach, they should look for teacher that can explain how śamatha is developed in the Mahamudra or Dzogchen Semde lineages.

    More importantly, the instructions are used to illustrate how the framework developed in the previous sections can be used to develop a version of practice fit for lay practitioners. This version does not water down traditional thresholds and does not require the cultivation of renunciation and sense restraint, which will hamper progress in those without the right conditions. The instructions make the following concessions:

    • Only the first dhyāna will be taught. Given that the second dhyāna and above are states of deep absorption, most people will find it difficult if not impossible to reach them outside a monastic or retreat setting that allows for the necessary conditions of renunciation and sense restraint.
    • It will be taught according to how Dzogchen understand the factors of the first dhyāna, which allows for the presence of sensory input and removes the need for sense restraint. This does not mean that a right livelihood, ethical discipline, and wisdom are not necessary, but that those qualities can be cultivated by lay practitioners much more easily than sense restraint.
      • Those interested in those versions should understand that they must cultivate the necessary qualities that are prerequisites and to connect with a living aural lineage that can guide them in the process.
    • There are no specific Dzogchen instructions other than those for the first dhyāna. Since this is a preliminary, the instructions do not contain details that reveal anything about the main practices. A few details from the proper instructions that do, concerning Dzogchen's understanding of the subtle body, are omitted.

    First, you must choose a suitable object of meditation. That can be the breath, a visualized image, a mantra or dharani, or a visualization of subtle anatomy. Not every wholesome and suitable object of meditation will be equally effective for everyone, and some are said to be much more effective because they are associated with powerful Buddhas, Bodhisattvas, or with more refined understandings of the body.

    While you are free to use any object of your choice, the guide uses recitation on the name of the Buddha Amitābha as the example object of meditation. Those unsure of what to choose are welcome to pick it. The reasons will be explained throughout the guide, but suffice to say that it is one of the most powerful objects of meditation that do not require any initiation or permission to practice.

    Now you can assume a comfortable posture with your back straight and your hands comfortably resting on your knees. Full lotus is ideal, but half lotus, Burmese, or even sitting on a chair with your feet flat on the ground are all acceptable as long as the spine is straight. The breathing is relaxed and natural, and is done lightly through the mouth, taking care that the mouth does not open too much or become dry. It is ok to breathe through the nose partially or fully if breathing through the mouth becomes uncomfortable. The breath should be allowed to slow down naturally as the meditation deepens without actively trying to force that outcome. Lightly rest at the end of inhalations and exhalations following the same principle and never holding the breath longer than is comfortable.

    Eyes are open and stare evenly into the space in front of you, and the rest of the senses are relaxed and open. Finally, we place our attention on the object of meditation. For the example of reciting the name of Amitābha, we mentally or vocally repeat the phrase "Namo Amitābha Buddha" (Homage to the Buddha of Infinite Light) or simply "Amitābha" if you feel unease at the longer phrase. Since this Buddha is often associated with light and with the sunset, it is also fine to visualize a beautiful warm light or sun, either at your heart, in front of you, or filling the whole space around you.

    When distractions arise, apply the understanding of one-pointedness described earlier: directly meet the distraction with your open senses and allow it to arise and subside on its own without trying attraction or aversion and return to the object. If distractions become too strong, you can temporarily increase the intensity of your attention on the object of meditation and relax it once distractions subside. This develops the factors of vitarka and vicāra, supported by the factor of samādhi.

    Continued engagement with the object of meditation will lead to the arising of the factors of prīti and sukha. My reason for picking Amitābha as the object of meditation is that it easily supports the developments of those factors. While explaining the full reasons are beyond the scope of this guide, it is enough to understand that the bodhisattva who became the Buddha Amitābha made a series of vows that he would fulfill before attaining enlightenment. Since we are told he became a Buddha, it is implied that all his vows were fulfilled and are active. Among those vows, listed as the 33rd vow in the Larger Sukhāvatīvyūha Sūtra, is the vow that anyone touched by his light will feel peace and happiness in their bodies and minds surpassing those of humans and devas.

    Because the first dhyāna is meant to replicate the experiences of a being in the first rūpa deva realm, this vow supports the natural arising of the factors of prīti and sukha. While other vows are only activated based on trust and faith, no such requirement is included in this vow. Those in doubt can test it out by simply following the instructions with an open mind. There is no need to consciously engineer states of bliss and pleasure by trying to force the arising of prīti and sukha. Instead, you simply sustain your attention on the recitation and the optional visualization and allow its natural effects to arise in your mind and body.

    The next vow in the list, the 34th vow, is also important, as it states that those who hear his name will gain the bodhisattva's insight into the non-arising of all dharmas. In other words, the recitation of the name of Amitābha supports the unified development of śamatha and vipaśyanā which is the basis of the Mahayana path.

    When all five factors are present in sufficient strength, the five hindrances (sensual desire, ill will, sloth and torpor, restlessness and remorse, doubt) are suppressed by them, and one gains the ability to remain on the object of meditation for any desired amount of time, the first dhyāna has been developed to its full strength. In the meantime, one trains on sustaining the factors of the first dhyāna, and gradually extending the sessions until physical and mental pliancy is fully developed. If one's session is going badly, it is better to end it early than to force oneself to continue and risk developing aversion to the practice.

    Once your practice of the first dhyāna is well-established, you can use the pliancy, flexibility, and stability of the mind in your pursuit of other Buddhist practices, the higher jhānas under proper guidance, or for your own benefit and the benefit of those around you. It is important to remember the warnings that without proper insight, the first jhāna by itself can create the conditions for further ignorance and attachment, and that such effects do not require a belief in rebirth or the afterlife to be recognized as potential dangers. Simply because those attachments are much more refined and subtle than coarser addictions does not mean they are without their own dangers and potential for misuse.
